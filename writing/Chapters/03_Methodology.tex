The first experiments were done in a simple bivariate setting. Two dimensional data, $(x,y)$ was generated from a bivariate Gaussian distribution, and then an SDE score-based diffusion model was trained to generate samples. Furthermore, a new marginal distribution $p^*$ was defined for the $y$ dimension and Jeffrey's rule was used to construct the new updated joint distribution $p^*(x,y)$ which became the new target for sampling. TDS, along with other simpler sampling approaches were tested out and compared. The intention of the bivariate toy example was to have a simple environment to test out TDS and other parts of the implementation. Along with getting a sense of how TDS performed and how it compared to the other techniques and which parameters and implementation details made the biggest difference. This experience could then be used for a more complicated problem with higher dimensional data such as images.

Four distinct sampling methods were used and analyzed. First, since the new joint $p^*$ is made up of two Gaussians, namely the new marginal and the conditional, it is itself a Gaussian distribution. This makes it tractable to sample exactly from the new joint which serves as a ground truth baseline. Furthermore, by the factorization of the joint, it follows that the conditional is also Gaussian so in practice, one can sample either directly from the new joint or sample from the updated marginal and then the conditional. Precisely, if
\[
    \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}\left(
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \mu_x \\
        \mu_y
    \end{pmatrix},
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \sigma_x^2 & \sigma_{xy} \\
        \sigma_{xy} & \sigma_y^2
    \end{pmatrix}
    \right),
\]
then
\[
    p(x\mid y)
    =
    \mathcal{N}\left(
        x;
        \mu_x + \frac{\sigma_{xy}}{\sigma_y^2}(y-\mu_y),
        \sigma_x^2 - \frac{\sigma_{xy}^2}{\sigma_y^2}
    \right).
\]
The exact Jeffrey baseline therefore samples $y\sim p^*(y)$ and then samples $x\sim p(x\mid y)$, which gives samples from $p^*(x,y)=p(x\mid y)p^*(y)$. This was used in the implementation, since it is simpler than explicitly constructing the full updated joint Gaussian and directly mirrors Jeffrey's rule: sample from the new marginal $p^*(y)$ and then from the preserved conditional $p(x\mid y)$.

The second method was importance resampling, where samples are drawn from the original model and then weighted by the ratio of the new and old marginals for the updated dimension, $\rho(y) = \frac{p^*(y)}{p(y)}$. Only the updated dimension is weighted since that is the only dimension that changed. The ratio is large when the $y$ value of a drawn sample has higher likelihood under the new marginal compared to the old one yielding a high weight. The post-weighting samples then behave like they came from $p(x,y) \cdot \frac{p^*(y)}{p(y)}$ which simplifies to the Jeffrey updated joint according to \cite{MatteiJeffreysRule}. This method is simple and exact in the limit of infinitely many samples, but its performance with a finite number of samples depends heavily on the overlap between the original and updated distributions. If the updated joint has little overlap with the original distribution, then most samples from the original model will have very low likelihood under the new marginal. As a result, the weights concentrate on only a small number of samples, or in the worst case effectively a single sample, while the remaining samples receive negligible weight. This leads to sample degeneracy and makes the resampled distribution a poor approximation of the Jeffrey updated joint. 

Thirdly, a naive guidance sampler was used. Rather than reweighting completed samples, this method modifies the score used during the reverse SDE. This new guided score is the score of the Jeffrey-updated marginal, that is $\nabla_{x_t}\log(p_\theta^*(x_t))$ which in practice is approximated by using the neural network. Let
\(\rho(y)=p^*(y)/p_\theta(y)\) denote the Jeffrey ratio, and let
\(\hat y_\theta(x^t,t)\) be the updated coordinate of the denoised estimate
\(\hat x_\theta(x^t,t)\). The guided score is defined as
\begin{equation*}
s_{\theta,\mathrm{NG}}(x^t,t)
=
s_\theta(x^t,t)
+
\gamma \nabla_{x^t}\log \rho(\hat y_\theta(x^t,t)).
\end{equation*}
The added term modifies the reverse drift so that the reverse process is steered toward directions in which the denoised estimate has higher likelihood under the Jeffrey ratio, thereby guiding generation toward the updated joint. Unlike TDS, however, this sampler does not use importance weights to correct for errors introduced by this approximate guidance.

The fourth and final method was Twisted Diffusion Sampler (TDS). Utilizing a sequence of proposals and weighting techniques applied to a set of particles at each step of the reverse process it guides the generation towards a desired distribution. The constructed particles form an asymptotic approximation of the target. Usually TDS is formulated for conditional generation, where the guidance appears through likelihood terms such as $p(y \mid x^0)$ and the time-depended twisting approximations. Through the application of Jeffrey's rule these conditional guidance terms are replaced by the Jeffrey distribution-guidance terms. This gives a sampler that guides particles toward the Jeffrey updated joint while using SMC weights to correct for errors in the approximate twisting functions.

These four methods were tested against eachother with two different Jeffery targets. First, the new marginal of $y$ was defined to be not too different from the original, such that the new joint distribution still overlapped with the original joint. Then, another new target marginal for $y$ was used, one with mean highly different from the original. This gave a target joint that did not overlap at all with the original. These two scenarios showcased the shortcomings of some of the methods and made it possible to distinguish methods that only work under mild distribution shifts from those that remain effective under more severe shifts.
